Internal briefing

Opportunity Engine

A system that watches public tender portals worldwide and surfaces the few legal-technology RFPs we could realistically win. It runs on a home server, it drafts the bid, and it never submits anything — a person approves every document before it leaves the building.

What it does

Governments and public bodies publish tens of thousands of tender notices a week. A few each month are legal-technology buys worth our time. Finding them by hand is the bottleneck, and putting an AI on every notice would be expensive for exactly that reason.

So the engine reads the feeds continuously, discards almost everything itself, and researches only what survives. What reaches us is a short ranked list — each entry with a score, an honest estimate of what bidding would cost us, and a draft response already written.

How it is wired

The home server. It all runs in Docker on a Lenovo box that is already switched on, next to the other services on it. That buys three things. Confidentiality: solicitation documents and our draft responses sit on our own disk, not in someone else's SaaS — PDFs are opened and OCR'd by the Stirling container on the same machine, never sent to an outside conversion service. Cost: the only running expense is the API calls the engine chooses to make. Resilience: the PDF container is heavy and gets switched off sometimes, so the engine treats it as optional — attachments queue, the dashboard says why, everything else keeps running.

n8n does the edges. On the way in, it watches the sources we can't poll directly — inboxes, alert emails, RSS from portals — and posts each notice into the engine's API, so adding a new source is a workflow rather than a code change. On the way out, the engine hands every alert to n8n, which decides where it goes: phone, Slack, email. The engine stays a single job — judge the opportunity and draft the bid — and n8n handles everything around it.

Why it stays cheap

Two filters, not one. The first is free and runs locally: is this legal technology at all, is it in a region we serve, is the deal big enough? Most of the volume dies there at no cost. Only survivors are read by Claude, which scores product fit, estimates what bidding would take, and writes the draft. Every rejection keeps its reason, so we can see what the filter is discarding and tune it.

Test run: 10 notices arrived → 5 dropped for free → 5 analysed → 2 scored too low → 3 waiting for us

The five dropped without cost were office furniture, a law-firm advisory panel, an undersized pilot, a country we don't bid in, and a job needing people on site. Half the volume never reached the API.

The line it does not cross

It may, on its own

  • Read public notices and attachments
  • Classify, score and reject opportunities
  • Research the buyer from the notice
  • Estimate effort, cost and win odds
  • Draft bids, proposals and deliverables
  • Keep the books and the audit trail

It may never, on its own

  • Submit a bid or send any message
  • Agree pricing or accept a contract
  • Spend money or buy anything
  • Sign or certify anything
  • Claim something about us we haven't verified
  • Deliver work to a client

That right-hand column is not a polite instruction the AI is asked to follow. Approval is a separate part of the system with no route into it from the AI side, and every decision writes a permanent record of what was approved. The same applies to what we claim: there is one profile of our capabilities, certifications and references, it currently ships blank, and the agents are forbidden from filling it in — so a tender demanding a certification we haven't recorded comes back flagged as a gap rather than quietly asserted.
